01 / Professional self-assessment

Learning to build, evaluate, and improve software.

A reflection on my computer science education, professional experience, and future direction.

My journey into computer science

My path to computer science began with years of technical field work involving equipment installation, maintenance, troubleshooting, and coordination with other technicians. That experience shaped how I approach software problems: understand the requirements, investigate failures, test a solution, and communicate what changed. Studying computer science at Southern New Hampshire University helped me apply those habits to programming, full-stack development, algorithms, databases, and software security.

My goal is to move into software development or data analytics. I am especially interested in building useful applications and learning to turn data into reliable information. My coursework has shown me that writing code is only part of the job; a good solution must also be understandable, maintainable, testable, and appropriate for the people using it.

Collaboration and decisions

In CS 250, studying Agile software development and the responsibilities of product owners, developers, testers, and Scrum masters helped me understand how teams coordinate priorities and respond to changing requirements. My professional background also taught me to communicate technical problems clearly and consider how a decision affects teammates and the people depending on a system. I bring that perspective to software design by documenting assumptions and choosing changes that can be reviewed and tested.

Communicating technical work

Across courses such as CS 255 Systems Analysis and Design and CS 230 Operating Platforms, I practiced translating requirements into technical documentation and explaining design choices for different audiences. The CS 499 code review and enhancement narratives extend that practice: they require me to explain not only what I changed, but why the change matters, how I evaluated it, and what limitations remain.

Algorithms and engineering trade-offs

Courses involving programming, artificial intelligence, and software testing strengthened my ability to compare possible solutions rather than accept the first implementation that works. For the capstone, I used birthday search, sorting, date calculations, and an in-memory lookup structure to examine how algorithm and data-structure choices affect behavior and efficiency. I also learned the importance of edge cases, including leap days and year boundaries. Passing local tests is useful evidence, but it is not a substitute for broader validation.

Software and database practices

In CS 465 Full Stack Development, I worked with Node.js, Express, Angular, REST APIs, and MongoDB through the Travlr Getaways application. For CS 499, I chose to enhance that earlier work instead of presenting an unrelated project. Adapting Travlr toward HappyBirthday.com gave me an opportunity to preserve the original architecture while developing birthday-oriented routes, a Mongoose data model, validation, indexes, and CRUD operations. In CS 320 Software Testing, I also learned why repeatable tests and clear failure reporting are essential to maintainable software.

Security, privacy, and responsibility

CS 305 Software Security reinforced that software quality includes identifying vulnerabilities and reducing unnecessary risk. Birthday information can be personal, so the capstone needs careful input validation, responsible error messages, and controls that prevent one user from accessing another user's records. My current work includes input validation, duplicate-handling logic, and session-based registration, login, and logout. However, the birthday API does not yet enforce per-user ownership authorization, so additional protection is required before public deployment. I want my portfolio to communicate that distinction clearly rather than overstate the application's security.

How the artifacts fit together

The three enhancements on this site represent one continuous learning process. The software engineering enhancement addresses application structure and functionality; the algorithms and data structures enhancement examines how birthday information is processed; and the database enhancement improves persistence and data integrity. Together they show my growing ability to revisit existing work, explain trade-offs, verify results, and identify what still needs improvement. The progression from Travlr to HappyBirthday.com also demonstrates how I can work across frontend interfaces, backend APIs, and persistent data rather than treating them as isolated pieces.

As I continue toward a software development or data analytics career, I plan to build on these foundations with more practice in Python, SQL, web applications, testing, and secure design. This ePortfolio is both evidence of my progress and a starting point for future projects.
